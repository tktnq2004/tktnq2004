SnapShot

  • Personal project
  • In progress

A camera app for iOS and Android that turns a photo of a person into a pose guide on the viewfinder, so whoever holds the phone can match the pose. A composition assistant shows where to put the person in the frame. Everything runs on the phone.

overview

You find a pose you like, the app cuts the person out of the photo and lays their outline over the camera. Your friend lines you up with it and shoots.

There is no server: the person is cut out on the phone, so photos never leave it. The first version (TakeASnap, 2025) sent every photo to a Python server on my home network. I rebuilt the app from scratch in November 2026 and renamed it SnapShot. It is a portfolio project, not on the app stores, tested on an iPhone 15 Pro Max and an Android emulator (Pixel 8a).

what it does

  • Shoot with a pose. A full camera (flash, timer, aspect ratio, grid, level, zoom from 0.5× to 5×, front and back, burst). The pose sits on the viewfinder; drag, pinch and rotate it to fit. Photos go to a SnapShot album without the overlay.
  • Make a pose from one of 32 sample poses in 5 groups or from your own photo. In a group photo, tap the person you want while it is still processing.
  • Edit a pose in four tabs: outline (thickness, colour, face lines), fill, background, and a brush to add or erase parts of the person with unlimited undo.
  • Composition assistant (in progress). Pick a technique such as rule of thirds, centred, low angle or selfie: guide lines and a target appear on the viewfinder, the pose snaps to the thirds, the tilt sensor tells you to level or tilt the phone, and the photo gets a short review after the shot. 16 lesson cards explain each technique, with a button to try it.

architecture

Expo Go cannot load custom native code, but it ships a WebView. The person is cut out by MediaPipe models (segmentation, face and 33 body landmarks) running in a hidden WebView, so one code path works on both iOS and Android. The models, about 42 MB at pinned versions, are downloaded once and then work offline.

The outline is drawn from vector contours with Skia, so thickness and colour change on the next frame. Gestures run on the UI thread with Gesture Handler and Reanimated; nothing calls setState while a finger is moving. Segmentation sits behind one Segmenter interface, so a later build can swap in Apple Vision or ML Kit without touching the rest.

problems i worked through

Cutting out a person without a server

Problem
The first version sent each photo to a Python server at a hard-coded address on my home network. Away from home, making a pose failed, and even the sample poses were cut out again every time they were picked.
What I did
Moved segmentation onto the phone: MediaPipe models in a hidden WebView, downloaded once and cached with a manifest. The 32 sample poses are cut out ahead of time, so they need no download at all.
Result
No server to run or pay for, and photos never leave the phone. A full test in airplane mode is still on my list.

Gestures that fought each other

Problem
In the first version, every new pinch reset the pose to its original size, every rotation snapped back to 0° and every drag jumped back to the centre, because the gesture code read stale values. Zooming the camera re-rendered the whole screen on every frame.
What I did
Rewrote the gestures with Gesture Handler and Reanimated shared values on the UI thread, measured them on real devices and fixed what the numbers showed: a 0.5 s delay before dragging, zoom that moved in steps, and, on Android, a JavaScript thread that fell to 15–25 fps while zooming. Zoom updates are now capped at about 30 per second.
Result
Dragging, pinching and rotating the pose holds 60 fps on the iPhone and 58–60 fps on Android.

A photo that matches the viewfinder

Problem
The first version cropped every photo to a fixed 15–75% of the preview height, so the saved photo did not match what you framed. Its "auto flash" switched the torch on and off from a random number.
What I did
Crop from the real size of the camera image instead of a share of the screen, and use the camera's own auto flash.
Result
What you frame with the pose is what ends up in the album.

Advice you can explain

Problem
A composition assistant is easy to fake with "the AI likes this photo". I have no GPU budget, and I wanted every hint to have a reason.
What I did
The assistant uses what the app already knows: where the person and the top of their head are, where the eyes and the 33 joints are, and how the phone is tilted. Rules from photography, written in TypeScript, turn that into one short hint at a time, such as too much space above the head or a knee cut by the frame.
Status
Technique guides, sensor hints, the review after the shot and the 16 lesson cards are in. Hints while you frame need a stream of camera frames that Expo Go does not provide, so I will measure speed, battery and heat first and decide from the numbers.

what's next

  • Finish the composition assistant, including the decision on hints while framing.
  • Find the horizon and leading lines in the photo, kept only if they are found in at least 80% and 70% of a test set.
  • Pick a technique automatically with a small model I train myself, shipped only if it beats choosing by hand.
  • A demo video, so the app can be seen without installing it.
